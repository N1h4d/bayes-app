Problem 1: Robot localization (Bayes filter)

Real-world use: robot vacuum cleaners, warehouse robots, self-driving cars, drones and phone navigation. They never know their exact position, so they keep a belief (probability for every place) and update it with Bayes’ rule after every noisy sensor reading. This is the basis of Monte Carlo localization and Kalman filters.

A robot is somewhere in a circular corridor of 20 cells. Some cells have a door (D), others a wall (W). The robot starts with no idea where it is (5% for every cell). Its door sensor is noisy and its wheels slip. Press Sense and the AI updates the belief with P(x | z) ∝ P(z | x) · P(x). Press Move and the belief shifts (with uncertainty). Repeat Sense and Move a few times: the belief concentrates on the true position, because only one place matches the whole pattern of doors and walls. (You can press Sense several times in a row: every reading is independent evidence. With a noisy sensor the AI may need many steps, which is realistic.)

Bars = belief P(x). D = door, W = wall, ▲ = real (hidden) position of the robot, numbers = cell index.

Two steps repeat forever: Sense = Bayes update (new evidence sharpens the belief). Move = prediction with the law of total probability: P(x′) = Σ P(x′ | x) · P(x), which makes the belief blurrier. Sensing reduces entropy, moving increases it.

Problem 2: A spam filter that learns (Naive Bayes classifier)

Real-world use: Gmail, Outlook and SMS spam filters, plus phishing and fraud detection. Spam filtering was one of the first large practical uses of Bayes’ rule, popularised by Paul Graham’s 2002 essay “A Plan for Spam”.

The classifier is trained on the small email dataset below. For every word it learns P(word | spam) and P(word | not spam), with Laplace smoothing so unseen words cannot break the maths. Type any message and the AI combines the evidence of all words (the “naive” assumption: words are independent). Then teach it: add your message to the training data and watch the model change.

Problem 3: AI Mind Reader (Akinator-style game)

Real-world use: medical diagnosis assistants, fault-finding in machines, and active learning all work this way: update beliefs with Bayes, then ask the most informative next question.

Think of one of the 12 animals below. The AI asks Yes/No questions. After every answer the probability of each animal is updated with Bayes’ rule. The AI does not pick questions randomly: it computes the information gain (expected drop in entropy) of every question and asks the best one. You may answer wrongly: the model already assumes an answer error rate (default 8%), so one mistake does not break the game.
